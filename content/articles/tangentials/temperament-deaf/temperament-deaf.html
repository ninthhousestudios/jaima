a digressive musical memoir and tuning theory primer

at my public school, 6th grade was the first year we had the opportunity to participate in special music classes: band, choir, or orchestra. so near the end of 5th grade during our general music class, we had the opportunity to try some instruments before choosing what class and instrument we wanted to start learning.

my first choice was to try the bassoon (fagotto in italian; though i didnt know it yet) but they didnt have an actual bassoon there that day, just a reed. the teacher offered to let me blow on the reed, but i didnt because that didnt seem very interesting to me. so i walked over the orchestra section, because i had thought about the violin. i tried the violin, and then the orchestra teacher said, “how about the viola”…i guess i must have tried and i just nodded and that is how i started playing the viola in sixth grade.

we had always listened to music in the car, but i hadnt really ever listened to it on my own until probably around 7th grade, when i got a computer in my room. those were the days of napster. i remember listening to “joga” by bjork, “kashmir” by led zeppelin. i got into black sabbath, and i really liked the first system of a down album. (i really do still like it, actually, even though im rarely in the mood. in the summer of 2016 i was a summer camp instructor for 4-6 years old…it *really* did not suit me and one week i played that album on the way to camp each day…my coinstructors riding in the van with me were…disturbed? reasonably)

i was in orchestra during all this time and i enjoyed it, but didnt really know that much about classical music at that point. sometime during 7th or 8th grade i read cry to heaven by anne rice, which really moved me in some ways, including stiffly, but it also got me interested in opera. i cant remember exactly which was my first opera. it was either a vhs of don giovanni at la scala with thomas allen and riccardo muti, or it was rinaldo (1711) by handel with david daniels, cecilia bartoli, and bernarda fink. rinaldo was what really got me though, because cry to heaven was about the castrati, which if youve never heard of that (i apologize, in advance):

around the year 1600 the pope decided that women could no longer speak in church…or sing in church. choral music at the time was very important in church services, and only having low voices would not do. there was some effort to import spanish falsettists, but then someone hit on a brilliant idea. they realized that all the prepubescent boys, who had soprano voices, would be just about trained by the time their voices changed…so, maybe, what if, say, the boy “fell off a horse” and the only way we could save his life was to cut his balls off…or maybe the horse stomped on them. hence, castrati, though at the time they were more often known as i musici. despite the procedure, the boys body wasnt entirely locked in a state of youth; sometimes the voice changed anyway, or became ugly where it was beautiful before. but, in enough cases, it succeeded, and the church now had “men” who could sing in the alto and soprano range. eventually the castrati came to be used outside the church, particularly on the stage in opera.

handel used many castrati in his operas. rinaldo has four roles for male characters who sing in an alto or mezzo-soprano register. i was quite taken by this and wanted to become a countertenor, as a male falsettist is called today. i even took singing lessons at one point during high school. i sang o mio babbino caro at my first recital, then a bass aria from the magic flute, o isis und osiris schenket at my second and final voice recital. i really wanted to be an opera conductor, but that didnt work out.

the practice of making castrati for the church continued until about 1860, even though they had fallen out fashion for opera by the late 1700s — mozart wrote for castrati; i dont believe that donizetti, bellini, rossini, or other such composers did. the last castrato, alessandro moreschi, died in 1922. he was a singer in the sistine chapel, and in 1902 and 1904 make a few solo recordings on some of the earliest recording equipment. this is him singing bach/gounoud’s ave maria

my immersion in rinaldo and don giovanni led me deeply into the world of opera in particular, and classical music in general. i became very passionate in some ways about playing. i say in some ways because i didnt always practice as i should have. i was caught up much more in the idea and romance of it than i was in the actual work necessary to do it well…as was a theme for me for a long time.

i took private lessons, played in a local youth orchestra, and even entered into the so-called young artists competition that was held by the orchestra. i submitted a recording, playing the piece lachrymae by benajamin britten, based on a song of john downland, for viola and piano. i was selected to compete at the live competition, one of six. i did not win, got sixth…apparently my c string was out tune (good thing it wasnt my g string!), which will be relevant later.

at this point i was seriously considering going to music school. i planned to do viola performance, but really wanted to be a conductor, preferably an opera conductor. i visited bard college, the new school in nyc, the new england conservatory in boston. i applied and auditioned at iu jacobs school of music, nec, and mcgill university in montreal. i was accepted at iu and mcgill, and chose to go to iu.

my first semester of college was quite intense. i got to play in the orchestra for an opera, verdi’s rigoletto, which i enjoyed, but near the end of the semester it just wasnt feeling right. i told me teacher that i was thinking about quitting, and he said, playing the viola is kind of like being married; that decided me! gtfo. it was a really good decision for me, undoubtedly the right one, for many reasons, but there is one i want to focus one:

the whole time i played the viola, there was one thing about it in particular that i just couldnt get, i just couldnt do right. that was: to play in tune. even to tune my instrument! i knew the principle of tuning my instrument and did it more or less, but i was never confident in it. in orchestra the oboe plays a4=440hz, and we tune our ‘a’ string, the highest string on the viola, to that pitch from the oboe, and from that we turn all the other strings, in “perfect” fifths…or at least some kind of fifths.

i was never ever confident that my ‘a’ string was correctly tuned. and it seemed like it should be easy…just match the note! its the same note! how hard could it be!?! well it was quite difficult for me, and then i could get the rest of my strings mostly in tune, but if someone had told they werent in tune, i wouldve automatically believed them because i had no faith in my ability to tune my instrument.

now i must have played mostly in tune, because i got into a competition, and i got into college, and i was often second chair in the viola section, but it wasnt because i could hear myself playing in tune or not…i basically played by sight; i.e., when i looked at the music i knew where my hand and fingers were supposed to go, and i mostly got it right i guess. i could not audiate, i could not hear the music “in my head”. if i look at music, i can tell you the name of the notes, but i have *no idea* how they sound just by looking at them.

what is going on? am i just tone deaf?

it turns out i am not tone deaf and there is actually a deep reason for all of these things.

i felt betrayed because i thought it was my fault that i couldnt hear in tune, but the word “temperament” in “12-tone equal temperament” literally means that its not in tune. yes, most of the music of the western world is actually not in tune. it turns out my ears and my brain do not do well with equal temperament. those just intonation chords just sound so good to me, and the 12tet so bad.

tuning theory

after that catharsis let us learn some pleasant sound arithmetic.

the basis of tuning theory derives from the natural phenomena of the way some things vibrate, specifically the way our voices vibrate. we are going to use the example of a string, because it is simpler to visualize. i find it very magical, partly in the sense that it is hard to believe. sometimes it is hard for me to believe that what i am about to tell you is true, but it is. it has been demonstrated scientifically and is not merely magic.

so, let us take a string. now, in the real world there are many factors and complications, but right now we are only considering the fundamentals of what is happening, so this is how it works in an ideal world.

so, we take a string and pluck it. the whole string vibrates, i.e., the string vibrates as a whole and this vibration creates a sound that we can visualize as a sine wave

it goes up and down. the number of times is goes up and down over a certain period of time is called the frequency. usually we use the unit hertz, Hz. 1 Hz is one cycle per second. in the case of our sine wave, one cycle is the time it takes to go from one crest to another. so if we have a frequency at 100Hz, there are 100 cycles in one second.

so let us say that the string as a whole vibrates 100 times a second, so the frequency that sounds is the frequency of 100Hz.

here is the magic: at the precise same time as the whole string is vibrating, each half of the string is also vibrating; i.e., each half of the string is vibrating independently of the whole string. each half of the string vibrates twice as fast as the string as a whole. so if the whole string vibrates at 100Hz, each half vibrates at 200Hz.

now, again, at the same time as this is happening, each third of the string is also vibrating. each third of the string vibrates three times as fast as the whole string, so each third of the string produces at tone at 300Hz.

this continues up to the point where it cannot physically do so. each fourth of the string vibrates, producing a tone at 400Hz, each fifth vibrates producing at tone at 500Hz, etc. here is a gif illustrating this

now, when we pluck this string, the tone we hear is said to have a frequency of 100Hz, which is called the fundamental. the fundamental is the frequency that the string itself is tuned to. all of these other tones that are produced as called upper partial tones, because they are tones above the fundamental, and they form a part of the sound. they are more commonly known as harmonics, and sometimes as overtones.

our vocal cords vibrate in this way and there are types of singing, called overtone singing, that allow a person to selectively emphasize a certain upper partial tone, which are often very high and sound “whistlely”. the overtone you hear is there if they sing the same pitch in their regular voice, but it just isn’t very obvious, because all of the upper partial tones blend together to create the sound you hear. in overtone singing, they are able to make one overtone stand out more than the others.

so all of these harmonics are present whenever we sing a note, or when a string vibrates, either by being plucked or with a bow. sometimes these upper partial tones can be distinguished by themselves, depending on the instrument, the situation, and the person who is listening, but usually we simply hear their combination, which is heard to have the frequency of the fundamental. so the string in our example is heard to have the pitch 100Hz, even though there are many of frequencies occurring at the same time.

different instruments have different timbres, which basically just means they sound different. the reason why is complex, but one important aspect to this is the overtones they produce. most instruments vibrate in such a way to produce harmonic upper partial tones as just described, i.e., their upper partial tones vibrate at frequencies that are integer multiples of the fundamental. but different instruments may have different intensities of certain harmonics. that is, some instruments might make more of the even harmonics, some instruments might have more pronounced odd harmonics. this is an important aspect to the quality of the sound they produce.

it is also a likely reason why i had trouble tuning my ‘a’ string. you might remember that in orchestra the oboe would play a4=440hz, and i would need to tune my viola’s ‘a’ string to that pitch…and i always had such trouble, and i always thought it meant i was tone deaf. but, the timbre of the oboe is very different from that of the viola, i.e., they sound different, duh!, but more specifically they produce different levels of certain harmonics. my hypothesis is that this is what made it so difficult. it is possible that my ears are more sensitive to overtones than they are to fundamentals2, and therefore it would have been very confusing to them to match a fundamental when the harmonics of the two pitches were so different; that is, the harmonics themselves are not different, but certain harmonics are emphasized in an oboe, and certain others are more important in forming the sound of the viola. im not sure exactly how to test this hypothesis, but i hope someday to have the chance.

this physical fact of vibratory nature and the resultant set of overtones is called the harmonic series, which is the basis for just intonation, and the perversion of which forms the foundation for 12-tone equal temperament.

intonation

what is intonation, exactly?

let us take the pitch 236Hz; is this pitch in tune?

(take a moment to consider; listen if you would like to this aria from handels rinaldo, “sulla ruota di fortuna”, sung by countertenor daniel taylor)

so, is 236Hz in tune? sorry, trick question.

the question doesnt make any sense because one pitch by itself cannot be in tune or out of tune. a pitch can only be in tune or out of tune relative to another pitch.

so to decide if a pitch is in tune, we need to compare it to another pitch. we can compare the frequencies of two pitches by showing them as a ratio, with one number on top of another, such as ³⁄₁. this is not a fraction, because we are not breaking anything apart here. we are comparing two pitches.

since Hz is a measure of how often something vibrates in one second, if both of the numbers in this example are in the units Hz, then 300Hz/100Hz means that the upper pitch vibrates 300 times a second and the lower pitch vibrates 100 times a second. we can reduce this ratio and cancel the unit, giving 3/1, which indicates that the higher pitch vibrates three times as fast as the lower pitch. said differently, every time the lower pitch vibrates once, the higher pitch vibrates three times.

in the most strict definition of just intonation, as long as two pitches can be related to each other using whole numbers, then those pitches are in tune. however, there is a general rule: the lower the numbers involved the more subjectively “in tune” the interval will sound; that is, lower numbers will sound better, higher numbers sound worse, when the ratio is reduced.3 for instance, according to our definition, the interval 236Hz/235Hz is in tune, but the numbers are very high and they probably wouldn’t sound very good together.

so how does this form this basis of just intonation in a practical sense?

let us go back to the harmonic series. the fundamental pitch we will call f. as we noted the second harmonic vibrates twice as fast as the fundamental, so the second harmonic vibrates at a rate of 2f. so the ratio between the second harmonic and fundamental is 2f/f = 2/1.

if we sounded at pitch at 100Hz and one at 200Hz, you would notice something interesting. the two pitches are “the same” in some sense. obviously they are different pitches, but they share some kind of identity with each other, something that neither share with, say, 150Hz.

we say that 200Hz is an octave above 100Hz. an octave of a note is a note that is basically the same note, just higher or lower. to go an octave above a pitch, we multiply the frequency by 2, as we saw above. since 100Hz is an octave below 200Hz, we can also go down an octave by dividing the frequency of a pitch by 2.

for example, if we want to go an octave below 100Hz, we divide by 2, which is the same as multiplying by 1/24. so the pitch an octave below 100Hz is 100Hz*(1/2) = 50Hz.

what about an octave above 200Hz? 200Hz*2 = 400Hz. how does this relate to our fundamental f=100Hz? 400Hz/100Hz = 4/1. so the pitch 400Hz is two octaves above the fundamental at 100Hz. in fact, this is the fourth harmonic, because the fourth harmonic is produced by one fourth of a string vibrating four times as fast as the fundamental.

octaves are great, but as you can see they are basically “the same” note. how do we get a different note? lets look at the third and fifth harmonics.

the third harmonic vibrates at 3 times the rate of the fundamental. the fifth harmonic vibrates as 5 times the fundamental. i hope you get the pattern now. the nth fundamental vibrates at n times the rate of the fundamental, and the number n is always an integer, meaning it is always a positive whole number.

if f=100Hz, then 3f=300Hz and 5f=500Hz. so the third harmonic has the ratio 3/1 and the fifth harmonic has the ratio 5/1. these pitches are actually quite high relative to the fundamental, so if we want to use the pitches to make music, it is helpful to put them in the same octave. recall that the ratio of an octave is 2/1. we can write the ratio 1/1 to mean a single pitch, and the pitch an octave above that as 2/1. so, basically if we have any ratio x/y, if 1/1 <= x/y <= 2/1, then the ratio x/y is within that octave. to make it easy, if you were to punch in x÷y into a calculator, if the answer were between 1 and 2, then the pitch is within the octave we are looking at.

so we can see that the third harmonic, 3/1, and fifth harmonic, 5/1, are not in the same octave, they are in higher octaves. let us octave reduce them so that we can more easily understand how the pitches relate to our fundamental.

since we need to bring them down octaves, we will multiply by 1/2 (i.e., divide by 2). 3/1*1/2 = 3/2. 3/2 is between 1/1 and 2/1, so it is now in the octave we are looking at.

now with the fifth harmonic: 5/1*1/2 = 5/2; this is greater than 2/1, so we need to bring it down another octave. 5/2*1/2 = 5/4. now this is in the octave we are looking at.

now we have four ratios, 1/1, 3/2, 5/4, and 2/1. lets see what the frequencies these ratios have when our fundamental is 100Hz. to do that, we simply multiply, just as we found the frequency of the octave (100Hz*2/1=200Hz).

so 100Hz*3/2=150Hz and 100Hz*5/4=125Hz. we can check are work: since 3/2 is an octave below 3/1, 150Hz*2=300Hz, shows that 3/2 is an octave below the third harmonic. likewise, 5/4 is two octaves below the fifth harmonic, so 125Hz*2*2=500Hz.

if we could play these pitches, they would be quite familiar to most musicians. if we played 150Hz and 100Hz together, this is very close to the “perfect fifth”. if we played 125Hz and 100Hz together, this is very close to the “major third”. i say “very close” because nowadays most people would understand “perfect fifth” and “major third” in relationship to 12-tone equal temperament, but the “perfect fifth” and “major third” of 12tet are not exactly these relationships.

however, the ratios 3/2 and 5/4 are just ratios. as you can see, they relate pitches together by whole numbers, and those numbers are very small, and give very pleasing sounds. if we must name then, we can call 3/2 a “just perfect fifth” and 5/4 a “just major third”.5

these ratios form the basis of music of western music, essentially. and their equal-tempered versions form the basis of most actual western music.

they form the basis because basically all the other intervals, or ratios, that we commonly use are combinations of these two ratios 3/2 and 5/4. and 2/1 of course, because most music use pitches in multiple octaves

(yes, we can use the seventh harmonic, eleventh harmonic, thirteenth harmonics as well, beyond, even, though this is the subject of modern music in just intonation. most of the music of the world has kept to ratios based on 1, 2, 3, and 5)

pythagorean tuning

pythagoras really liked the number 3, so he based a whole tuning system on the number 3, specifically on the ratio 3/2. what he did was start with a fundamental, 1/1, and go up and down by 3/2 ratios until he had approximately 12 pitches, which are in fact very similar to the pitches we use today, though different in their specifics.

as we do this exercise, you might be reminded of the circle of fifths. however, the ratio 3/2 does not form a circle, it forms an infinite spiral up and down, because the ratio 3/2, taken up or down, no matter how many times, will never form an octave of any previous pitch.6

two unfortunate digressions

before we do this exercise we need to make two unfortunate digressions.

the first concerns names of notes. there is something magical about the system of using the letters A,B,C,D,E,F,G as well as sharp (#) and flat (b). it works really well when applied to just intonation and the two just tuning systems i will be showing you. im not entirely sure how to explain why, but it just does, as you will see; it is very useful.

one important thing to be aware of though, if you are only used to thinking of note names relative to equal temperament: in 12tet we say that C# and Db are “enharmonic”. the black key just to the right of C on a piano represents both notes. this is not the case in just intonation. in the two tuning systems i will be showing you, C# and Db are different pitches! likewise, F# and Gb are different, E and Fb are different, B# and C are different! in just intonation, a different name gives a different pitch.

the second important digression: when we look at pitches as a ratio, sometimes it can be hard to tell how two different ratios are related; which one is higher? are they close together or far apart? we could convert them to Hz, based on a specific value for 1/1. for example, say 1/1=100Hz and we want to look at the ratios 5/4 and 81/64. in this case 100Hz*5/4=125Hz and 100Hz*81/64=126.56Hz. you can see these two ratios are actually pretty close, but that is hard to see just by the ratios.

at the same time, if we want to compare, it would be annoying to have to convert to Hz all the times, especially since the 1/1 may change. the frequency of 5/4 relative to 300Hz will be different than when related to 100Hz.

so we are going to use a system called “cents”. this system is very useful, and also unfortunate is some ways. it is unfortunate because it is based on 12-tone equal temperament, so when we look at ratios in terms of cents, 12tet intervals have whole numbers, and just ratios have weird numbers, making it seem like the just ratios are weird. but this is because the system of cents was devised based on 12tet. so the space of one octave is 1200cents, and thereby each half-step is 100cents. so if C is 1/1, then equal-tempered E is four half-steps, or 400cents above C.

the formula for converting a ratio to cents will be in this footnote7. as an example, the two ratios above is cents are as follows, 5/4=386.3 cents; 81/64=407.8 cents. so remember that the equal-tempered major third (C to E, for example) was 400 cents. you can see that these two just ratios are very close to the equal-tempered major third. you can also see what i say this is unfortunate. to me the ratio 5/4 sounds very good and the equal-tempered major third doesnt sound good at all…but 400 appears like a nicer number than 386.3…but, again, that is simply because cents was devised based on 12tet. when later we look at 12tet, you will see that it cant truly be described using ratios.

a 12tet half-step is actually quite a large interval. most humans can probably distinguish between pitches 10 cents apart. that is, if i played two pitches that were 10 cents apart, most people would be able to hear that they are different. even pitches 5 cents apart can be distinguished. people who have good ears, or good training, like piano tuners and others can distinguish 2, and sometimes even 1, cent differences. 12tet actually leaves out a lot of pitch possibilities!

5-limit tuning

the term 5-limit means that we will be using only the 2nd, 3rd, and 5th harmonics to construct our tuning system.

pythagorean tuning can be considered a 3-limit tuning system, because it uses only the 2nd and 3rd harmonics. remember that 3/1 is the third harmonic, which we octave reduce to 3/2. when we go up by a 3/2 ratio from 3/2, that is 9/4. 9/1 is technically the ninth harmonic of 1/1. but it is also the third harmonic of 3/2, octave-reduced. basically, we constructed a tuning system using the third harmonic of a bunch of pitches. we found the third harmonic of 1/1, then them third harmonic of that, etc. when we divide by 3/2, we are finding the pitch for which 1/1 is a third harmonic. thus, C is the third harmonic of F.

as you can see, when we only use the 3rd harmonic, the ratios become very large very quickly, and while these large ratios are strictly speaking “in tune”, because the two pitches of the ratio can be related by whole numbers, most of them dont really sound that great.

so let us include the fifth harmonic. we will start by taking the fifth harmonic of 1/1, then finding the pitch for which 1/1 is the fifth harmonic. then we can combine the third and fifth harmonics, i.e., find the fifth harmonic of the third harmonic, the third harmonic of the fifth harmonic, etc. and see if we can find a pleasant sounding tuning system.

since note names work really well with this system we will use them to help us. in terms of note names the 5/4 ratio is a “major third”, e.g, C-E, D-F#, and the 3/2 is a “perfect fifth”, e.g, C-G, D-A.

so let us start with C=1/1. we will be keeping G=3/2 and F=4/3, because these are the simplest ratios in that system, even simpler than 5/4. we may or may not keep some other ratios from the 3-limit system, depending on what we find.

so going up from C=1/1 by 5/4 gives E=5/4=386 cents. this is a much simpler ratio than 81/64, so we will keep this.

going down from 1/1 by 5/4 gives us 4/5. let us bring that into the octave we are working in by multiplying by 2, giving us Ab=8/5. that is much simpler than 128/81, so we will keep Ab=8/5.

what if we find the fifth harmonic of the fifth harmonic of 1/1? 5/4*5/4=25/16, which we can call G#. this is definitely better than the 3-limit G#=6561/4096, but we are only wanting to find a 12 note system at this point, and since G# and Ab inhabit approximately the same space, we are going to use the Ab we already found.

now what about below? 8/5*4/5*=32/25 which is Fb. this is a fine ratio, but we are not needing an Fb at the moment.

if we go up again by 5/4 from G# we get B#, and down again from Fb we get Dbb, neither of which is in our system at the moment. so let us start combining third and fifth harmonics now.

take 3/2 and go up by 5/4: 3/2*5/4=15/8, G up a 5/4 is B. so B=15/8. our 3-limit B is 243/128. what if we take 5/4 and go up a 3/2? up a fifth from E is B, so it is the same. so the fifth harmonic of the third harmonic is the same as the third harmonic of the fifth harmonic, because 3*5=5*3. so as long we are going in the same direction, the order doesnt matter.

now let us go down by a third harmonic and a fifth harmonic. 1/1÷3/2÷5/4=1/1*2/3*4/5=8/15, which is less than 2, so multiply by 2 to bring it up an octave gives 16/15. C down a fifth is F, down a third is Db, so Db=16/15. this is a much better ratio than 256/243.

how about up a 3/2 and down a 5/4? 3/2*4/5=12/10=6/5. up a fifth and down a major third gives C to G to Eb. so we will take Eb=6/5.

down a 3/2 and up a 5/4: 2/3*5/4=10/12=5/6, 5/6*2=10/6=5/3. down a fifth is F, up a major third is A. so we will take A=5/3.

here is what we currently have then

since D is a fifth up from a fifth up from C, let us keep the same ratio D=9/8. we could go down two fifths, C to F to Bb and up a third to D, which would give 10/9, which is a fine ratio. but we will keep 9/8 as it is simpler.

what about Bb? well, Bb is a minor third up from G, so let us take 3/2*6/5=18/10=9/5. our 3-limit Bb was 16/9, which isnt a very complicated ratios, but i think we will still choose 9/5 because it is simpler.

similarly, F# is a major third above D. so 9/8*5/4 is 45/32. these numbers are pretty high, but the 3-limit F# and Gb are both much more complicated. we could find Gb by going down a 5/4 from Bb, 9/5*4/5=36/25, which is certainly simpler than 45/32. for the sake of the exercise we will stick with F# rather than Gb. however, both of these would be the most complicated ratios in our system, which seems fitting because they hold the “tritone” position, which is known to have a dissonant sort of sound. in just intonation, especially when we add in 7th, 11th, and 13th harmonics, there are actually quite a few ratios that can be used as a “tritone”, which i find very interesting.

that brings us to what might be considered the “standard” or almost simplest 5-limit tuning system. take any frequency for 1/1 you want and you can see what the frequency would be of any pitch.

as you can see, our ratios are much simpler than most in the 3-limit system and these intervals will sound very nice.

is there a problem with this?

if this were being explained by a western musician or music theorist, at this point they would probably say something like, “but, wait, theres a problem”.

so, is there a problem with just intonation?

that depends on your goals, your aesthetic, musical values, and to some degree on your equipment. there is no inherent problem with just intonation.

indian classical music, which is a vast and intricate system, in based on just intonation. i hope someday to learn about this music from a practitioner, and to learn how the indians understand intonation, but i will describe my limited understanding.

the basic instrument of indian classical music is the tanpura, this beautiful instrument here:

as we know, in order to play music in tune, with whole number ratios, we need to have certain pitch, a 1/1, to which to tune all of our other pitches.

that is the function of the tanpura is indian classical music. the 1/1 is called ṣaḍja8, or sa for sort. this is the same idea as the “tonic” in western music, or the “key” that one is in.

however, indian classical music doesnt “change keys” within the space of one piece. their system is based on ragas, which is a complex topic. a raga is often described as a “scale”, which may have different patterns ascending and descending, but this is only the most simplistic explanation. unfortunately i do not truly know more about it than it.

the tanpura usually has four strings, with the middle two tuned to 2/1, an upper sa, the lowest string tuned to 1/1 sa, and then the first may be tuned to different notes depending on the raga, but most often it will be 3/2, the “dominant”, pañcam or pa.

the indian system of note names is a moveable solfeggio. so sa is always the tonic, pa is always the dominant. therefore, it doesn’t particular matter what the frequency of the 1/1 sa is; the performer can choose based on the instrument, or his or her voice.

the tanpura is really a magical instrument. it is constructed and played in such a way that the harmonics can be clearly heard. what is more, a skilled tanpura player can tune the instrument in such a way that the overtones that one hears match very well with the pitches the solo instrument is playing. you can read more about the specifics of how tanpura works here.

so just intonation is not inherently problematic, and in fact the whole tradition just described is based on just intonation.

however, there are so uses for which just intonation can be challenging, and this most often comes down to three facts:

  1. wanting to “change key” in the middle of a piece of music, i.e., wanting to change the pitch that is used to define all of the other pitches

  2. wanting to regularly play multiple pitches at once, i.e, chords, and have complex harmonies

  3. being limited by instrument design

some people will say that indian classical music doesnt have harmony, which i disagree with. the tanpura always has at least two pitches and the performer will be most often sounding a third pitch. in fact, harmony at its most basic is how intonation works. also consider that the fundamental, fifth harmonic, and third harmonic together make a just major triad, so even one string of a tanpura by itself can sound a major chord.

however, it is truly that complex harmony, and using chords based on different scale degrees is not part of this system.

just intonation itself is not necessarily an issue for complex harmony, but thats were number 3 comes in. somewhere along the line in europe someone made a keyboard with 12 notes per octave, and in some sense this is what really limits just intonation in western music.

when we get a keyboard with 12 notes per octave, and we want to change keys, and we want complex harmony all together, things become difficult. let us consider why.

let us say we have a harpsichord that is tuned to our 5-limit scale in this manner:

then we know we can play a major chord based on C. what about F? we can find the difference between a ratio by dividing the higher by the lower. so what is the distance between A and F, which we want to be a just major third? 5/3÷4/3 => 5/3*3/4 = 15/12 = 5/4, which is a just major third, and we know F to C is a 3/2.

we also know G to B is 5/4, since that is how we found B, and that G to D is a 3/2. so we have the chords I, IV, and V. what about ii, a minor chord based on D? F to D => 4/3*8/9 = 32/27, which is a 3-limit minor third, acceptable. what about D to A? 5/3*8/9 =40/27=680 cents. oh! we want D to A to be 3/2 in order to make a chord, but D to A is 22 cents smaller than a 3/2. while 40/27 is technically in tune, it doesnt sound very good. it is called a “wolf-fifth”. basically what this means is that with a keyboard instrument with this tuning, we cannot reasonably play a minor ii chord.

suppose we want to modulated to the V for a bit, and in doing so we wanted to use the dominant chord in the dominant, so a major chord based on D. well, we know D to F# is a 5/4 ratio, but again, D to A is 40/27, so this is not viable.

so this is the issue: with only twelve keys, if we tune to a just scale, we have very limited options for what other keys we could change to; well, none others actually, since even in the key of 1/1, you dont have all the chords based on the scale degrees.

we could build an instrument that has many more keys than just 12 so that we can add extra pitches. then we could make a key for 3/2 ratio above D, call it A+, and then we could have our ii and dominant-dominant chord. and we could keep adding keys until we had all the pitches we wanted.

in fact, a 16-century inventor actually tried something like this, though it wasn’t exactly based on just intonation (you can watch a 12tone video about it here). obviously that didn’t catch on, and over the centuries many other people have tried a wide variety of things in order to make keyboards, chords, and key-changing work much better with just intonation. alas, to no avail. the 12 key system, i suspect, is simply too convenient…and difficult enough to use in practice that adding more keys probably wouldnt be very pleasant.

so what do we do if we want to change keys and play complex harmonies with 12 keys?

this is where the idea of temperament comes in. in temperament, we “temper”, i.e., slightly change, our intervals from the just ratios to allow for greater flexibility. so a tempered interval is not technically in tune, but is there a way it can be tempered that sounds acceptable and not too terrible? some people think so.

for example, meantone temperaments keep the 5/4 major third, but slightly reduce the 3/2 ratio. on a keyboard tuned in this way, one can play in about six keys. in should be noted that in meantone temperament we still do not have “enharmonic” notes, i.e, in meantone, C# and Db are different. the consequence of these two things is that there is usually an interval, often G# to Eb, which is a fifth in theory, but in practice is a “wolf-fifth”. there are many different varieties of meantone, but they all follow this basic idea.

then there are the so-called well-temperaments. this name comes from bach’s well-tempered clavier. “tempered” here means that it is not in just intonation, which bach surely knew all about. some have suggested that well-temperament was 12-tone equal temperament, but i doubt it, in accord with kyle gann. one reason is that there is another way to temper the tuning that allows one to use all twelves notes in both major and minor keys, yet isnt equal temperament.

essentially, whereas meantone temperament changed all the fifths, well-temperament changes only some of the fifths. that is, some of the fifths are 3/2 ratios, while some are not. the consequence is that some major thirds are 5/4 ratios, while some are not. however, properly done, all the keys have triads that are “bearable”, i.e., that sound good enough to make music with them.

often people will speak of different keys having different feelings. i am dubious of this in 12tet, because, as we will see shortly, in 12tet, regardless of which key, all intervals have the same relationship with each. of course, for people like jacob with perfect pitch, this makes sense, but for people like me, who cant tell the difference between F and A, for example, without reference, it doesnt make sense.

however, in well-temperament is makes perfect sense that some keys would have a different quality than other keys, because they literally are different. one key has a justly tuned triad, another key does not.

im guessing that most recordings of the well-tempered clavier today are in 12tet. i would love to hear one in a well-temperament.

that brings us to the final step on our journey, our standing tuning system in modern western music:

12 tone equal temperament

the idea of equal temperament is that we take the octave and split it into an certain number of equal parts. since the 12-keyed keyboard had long been standard, 12 equal parts seemed to be the thing to try. of course, you can split the octave into however many equal parts as you want using the same method.

in 12tet, there are 12 “half-steps”. let us first try taking our 5-limit “half-step”, 16/15, and see what happens when we go up by 12 of them. since we multiply to go up by a certain interval, we are going to multiply 16/15 by itself 12 times. in other words

this is where the use of cents is unfortunate. cents was basically designed for 12tet, so E is 400 cents, and F is 500 cents, etc., which makes it seem like these are nice intervals…but they arent really.

the advantage of 12tet, for those who want it, is that one can change keys at will and play complex harmony…if you think the sound is bearable. apparently, most people think the sound is reasonable, and find something to like in an equal-tempered triad and any equal-tempered chord you can name or invent.

i, however, do not.

temperament deaf

i dont think 12tet sounds very good. once i was exposed to the beauty of a just triad, which sounds very good and right to me, 12tet just doesnt have it. of course, i listen to music in 12tet, because it is almost impossible not to nowadays, though i try not to listen too closely. its not so much that is sounds “out of tune”, it simply has a quality that i find grating. piano and guitars, especially when playing chords, sound very bad to me. it is really a qualitative dislike, rather than an awareness of specific pitches being out of tune. i can hear generally patterns in a harmony, but my ears dont do well with multiple notes played at once, especially complex chords…i just cant make sense of them sonically.

one of the possible reasons it doesnt sound good to me is that my ears might be more sensitive to upper partial tones. as we know, any pitch with harmonic upper partial tones actually contains a major triad, and if we play a just major triad then those harmonics are reinforced by congruent fundamentals, which also have harmonics that align well with all of the pitches.

if we play C-E-G on a 12tet piano, the C will produce a major triad in its harmonics, but the E that is played wont be in line with the C’s harmonics. same for the G. so whereas in just intonation harmonics align, in 12tet harmonics do not align, and this i believe is one reason that i do not appreciate 12tet.

so that is the story of why i thought i was tone deaf, and how i discovered that i am simply temperament deaf, and can, in fact, appreciate natural intonation.

further resources

some good books about tuning theory

The Arithmetic of Listening by Kyle Gann - i learned primarily from this book. gann talks about just intonation as well as equal temperament, as well as related systems. there are some systems that are called equal-divisons-of-the-octave (edos). they are similar to equal temperament in that they divide the octave into equal parts, but they dont use all of the possible notes. the idea is approximate just intonation, for instance, if you use 53 or 55 equal divisions of the octave, there are some intervals that are very close to just intervals. this book is a really good introduction to tuning theory.

Genesis of a Music by Harry Partch - partch was a truly american composer. he was born in 1901 and was something of piano virtuoso in his youth, until he found on the sensations of tone by helmholtz, which introduced him to just intonation. not long after he decided to only write music in just intonation, and consequently he built many of his own instruments in order to play the music he devised. partch’s original instruments are housed at the university of washington in seattle, where they are still played. there are two other sets of copies of his instruments that have been built and performed upon.

On the Sensations of Tone by Hermann von Helmholtz. this is a scientific exposition of the upper partial tones. a very thorough account of experiments into the nature of sound, with large sections on tuning theory.
